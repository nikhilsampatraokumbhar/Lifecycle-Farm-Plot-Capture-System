Screen 3 · 7:34 am, at the club

Being able to change the plan beats making a better one

The order set last night is already wrong, two farmers are at the market, one hasn't sown. So this screen isn't a route, it's a plan that costs thirty seconds to redo. I originally designed a clever route optimiser here and cut it: any minute spent making the plan smarter is worth less than a second spent making it easier to fix. The club leader knows who's home. The app should ask him, not out-guess him.

Screen 5 · The screen that decides whether the day works

Design for the boring answer

Most farmers sow most of their field. That answer is one tap and twenty-five seconds. No map, no walking, no typing. Everything clever in this app is for the five farmers a day who need it. If the common case took four minutes instead, twenty farmers wouldn't fit in a day and the whole thing collapses. One question, two buttons, both reachable with a thumb.

Screen 9 · Two seconds, not two minutes

The app pushes back, but it doesn't block

Over 20% short, so the app asks for a reason. A request with a stated cause, not an error. Six taps, no typing. These six aren't decoration: "no water" is a procurement forecast problem, "land dispute" is a legal one, "no seed" is our own supply failure. Same 0.6 acres, three completely different responses from three different teams, and the difference costs him one tap.

Screen 11 · Sixty seconds that save a settlement

The farmer signs it himself

The dispute doesn't happen today. It happens five months from now, at settlement, with money on the table, when Suresh says that was never my land, and it's his word against a technician who's since visited four hundred farms. You lose that argument every time. Sixty seconds now is the cheapest prevention there is. The quieter effect matters more: if the farmer is watching, the capture can't be faked. "Not here" is deliberately available, because a missing farmer must never cost us the capture.

Screen 13 · Error state · weak GPS

Never block a man alone in a field

No signal, no supervisor to phone, thirteen farmers left. If the app refuses his work he has no way forward, so he learns the app is an obstacle to get around, and everything he captures afterwards is worth less. So: save it, label it honestly, let the office deal with it. "Save and go on" is the primary button, deliberately. A record with a warning on it beats no record and a technician who's stopped trusting the tool.

Screen 14 · Error state · overlapping claims

What the app refuses to do

It does not ask "which one is correct?" That question would make a man with a smartphone the judge of a land dispute between two people who will still be neighbours in twenty years, and both will blame him. That's not a software problem, it's a village problem needing authority, both parties and probably a land record. The app's entire job is to notice it, keep both claims, and escalate. Knowing where the app's authority ends is a design decision.
